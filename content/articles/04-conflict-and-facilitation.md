# Conflict & Team Facilitation

*Module 04 · Speed Leas' 5 levels of conflict · 7 min read*

## Key takeaways

- Conflict is normal in a team. The question is **how serious** it is.
- Adkins uses Speed Leas' **five levels of conflict**: problem to solve, disagreement, contest, crusade, world war (Adkins, 2010; Leas, 1985).
- The **response depends on the level**. At the lower levels, the coach helps the team solve the conflict itself.
- Retrospectives in **five stages** (Derby & Larsen, 2006) and clear **working agreements** turn conflict into constructive dialogue.

---

## 1. Why conflict is not the enemy

Teams that never disagree are often teams where people do not feel safe to speak. Edmondson defines team **psychological safety** as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, and links it to team learning (Edmondson, 1999). A healthy team can disagree about ideas without attacking people.

Tuckman's stages also show that conflict is part of a team's growth: after *forming* comes *storming* (Tuckman, 1965). The coach's job is not to remove all conflict, but to keep it **useful**.

## 2. The five levels of conflict

Speed Leas described five levels of conflict, and Adkins applies them to agile teams (Leas, 1985; Adkins, 2010). Each level raises the stakes.

| Level | Name | What it looks like | Typical language |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Problem to solve** | People disagree but focus on the problem and share information openly | "Let's look at the data together." |
| 2 | **Disagreement** | Self-protection starts; people talk more cautiously and in general terms | "Some people here don't care about quality." |
| 3 | **Contest** | Winning becomes more important than solving; people take sides | "We're right and they're wrong." |
| 4 | **Crusade** | The other side is seen as the problem; the goal is to protect one's own group | "They will never change. They should leave the team." |
| 5 | **World war** | The goal is to hurt or eliminate the other side, whatever the cost | "Either they go or I go." |

*Sources: Adkins (2010), based on Leas (1985). The example sentences were written by the project team.*

## 3. De-escalation: what the coach does at each level

| Level | Coach's response |
| --- | --- |
| 1 · Problem to solve | Let the team work it out. Encourage collaboration and help the group reach consensus if needed. |
| 2 · Disagreement | Support the team so it can solve the problem itself: listen to each side, help people say what they really need. |
| 3 · Contest | Slow things down and bring the discussion back to facts and shared interests; help the sides negotiate an agreement they can both accept. |
| 4 · Crusade | Talk with each side separately and carry messages between them ("shuttle diplomacy"); bring the conflict back down a level before bringing people together. |
| 5 · World war | Do whatever is needed to prevent people from harming one another, including separating them and involving leaders or HR. |

*Source: Adkins (2010), based on Leas (1985). Wording adapted by the project team; check against the book before publishing.*

Two general rules from the research (section 7):

- **At the lower levels, the coach helps the team solve the conflict itself.** A good sequence is to listen to each person privately, then facilitate a conversation on shared interests that ends in a **working agreement**.
- **Blaming in public or escalating too early tends to raise the level.**

## 4. Facilitation tools for constructive dialogue

### The diamond of participatory decision-making

Kaner et al. describe group decisions as a diamond: a **divergent** phase (many ideas), a **"groan zone"** where the group struggles to understand different views, and a **convergent** phase (choosing). Many conflicts happen in the groan zone. The facilitator's job is to help the group *through* it, not skip it (Kaner et al., 2014).

### The five stages of a retrospective

Derby and Larsen propose five stages for a retrospective (Derby & Larsen, 2006). Each stage helps keep difficult conversations constructive:

| Stage | Purpose | How it helps with conflict |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1. **Set the stage** | Open the meeting, recall the goal, agree how we will talk | The team states or reviews its **working agreements** (for example "one person speaks at a time", "talk about the problem, not the person") |
| 2. **Gather data** | Collect facts and events from the sprint | Discussion starts from shared facts, not opinions about people |
| 3. **Generate insights** | Look for patterns and causes | The group looks at causes in the system, not at who is to blame |
| 4. **Decide what to do** | Choose one or two actions | The conflict ends in a concrete agreement or experiment |
| 5. **Close the retrospective** | Summarise and thank people | People leave with a shared conclusion |

*Sources: Derby & Larsen (2006) for the five stages; the "How it helps with conflict" column is a summary written by the project team.*

### Working agreements

A working agreement is a short list of rules the team writes **for itself** about how it works and talks together. Because the team writes the rules, people accept them more easily than rules imposed from outside. When a conflict comes up, the coach can point to the agreement instead of judging a person. *(Team summary based on research document, section 7.)*

## Practical example: two developers clash over code reviews

**Situation.** Two developers keep fighting in code review comments. Reviews take days, and other team members avoid both of them (research document, section 9).

**Diagnose the level.** The comments have become personal ("you always…") and others are starting to take sides. This sounds like **level 2, moving towards level 3**.

**What the coach does.**

1. Talks to each developer **privately** to understand their concerns (facilitator and coach stances).
2. Facilitates a conversation between them on **shared interests**: both want good code and fast reviews.
3. Helps them write a **working agreement** for code reviews, for example "comment on the code, not the person" and "reply within one working day".
4. Brings the topic to the next retrospective so the whole team can adopt the agreement.

**Avoid:** criticising one developer in front of the team, or escalating to their manager at the first sign of tension. Both tend to raise the level (research document, section 7).
