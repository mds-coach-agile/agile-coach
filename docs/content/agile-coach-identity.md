# The Agile Coach Identity

*Module 01 · Beyond the Scrum Master title · 6 min read*

## Key takeaways

- An Agile Coach helps teams and organisations work in an agile way and **keep improving on their own** (Adkins, 2010).
- The coach is a **catalyst, not a manager**: no authority over people, no assigning work, no doing the team's job.
- The coach looks at the **whole system** around the team, not only at what happens inside the sprint.
- Success means the team **no longer needs its coach** (Adkins, 2010).

---

## 1. What an Agile Coach is

An Agile Coach helps people find better ways of working, then steps back so the team becomes autonomous (Adkins, 2010). Davies and Sedley describe the coach as someone who helps a team adopt and improve agile practices by working *alongside* it, not by imposing them (Davies & Sedley, 2009).

In a normal week, a coach might:

- teach agile principles and practices (Scrum, Kanban, XP) when the team does not know them yet;
- facilitate events and workshops so the group reaches its own decisions;
- mentor Scrum Masters, Product Owners and managers who are learning their role;
- ask open questions so people find their own answers;
- observe the team and give feedback;
- work with leaders to remove obstacles outside the team's control.

These activities match the four coaching stances of the Agile Coaching Institute: teacher, mentor, facilitator and professional coach (Spayd & Adkins, 2011). Module 03 explains each one.

## 2. Catalyst, not manager

In chemistry, a catalyst makes a reaction happen faster without being used up by it. That is a useful picture of the coach: the coach helps change happen in the team, but the change belongs to the team. *(Metaphor written by the project team.)*

A manager and a coach can work with the same team, but they hold very different positions:

| | Manager | Agile Coach |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Authority over people | Hierarchical | None: influences and supports |
| Responsible for | Results and people management | Teams that improve on their own |
| Typical move | Decides, assigns, follows up | Asks, facilitates, makes patterns visible |

*Source: research document, section 3 (Adkins, 2010; Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).*

**In short:** a manager is responsible for getting results *through* the team. A coach is responsible for helping the team become able to get results *by itself*. *(Summary written by the project team.)*

## 3. Why the coach has no authority, on purpose

Having no hierarchical authority is not a weakness of the role. It is what makes the role work.

- **Self-management needs space.** The Scrum Guide describes Scrum Teams as "self-managing, meaning they internally decide who does what, when, and how" (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). If the coach decided for the team, the team would never learn to decide.
- **People speak more openly to someone who does not judge their performance.** Retrospectives and experiments only work when people feel safe to speak (Edmondson, 1999; see Module 04).
- **Influence lasts longer than orders.** A team that found its own solution keeps using it when the coach leaves. *(Team summary based on Adkins, 2010.)*

A common misconception is "the Agile Coach is the team's boss". It is wrong: the coach has no hierarchical authority (research document, section 10).

## 4. A systemic perspective

Many problems a team has do not start inside the team. A coach therefore looks at the **system** the team works in: other teams, leaders, processes, incentives and the organisation's rules.

Example: a team keeps missing its Sprint Goal. Looking only inside the sprint, you might blame estimates. Looking at the system, the coach may notice that urgent requests from another department arrive every week without going through the Product Owner. That obstacle is outside the team's control, so the coach works with leaders to remove it (research document, section 2). *(Example written by the project team.)*

Appelo makes a similar point for managers in an agile organisation: they should "manage the system, not the people" (Appelo, 2011). The coach helps leaders do exactly that.

## 5. The goal: a team that no longer needs its coach

Adkins describes the aim of coaching as a team that no longer needs its coach (Adkins, 2010). In practice, the coach's help changes as the team grows:

| Team situation | What the coach does more of |
| --- | --- |
| New team, new to agile | Teaching: short trainings on events and artifacts, then stepping back |
| Team in conflict, finding its way | Facilitating: helping the team talk and agree |
| Experienced team | Coaching: asking questions, letting the team lead |

*Source: research document, sections 6 and 9 (Tuckman, 1965; Adkins, 2010).*

## Practical example: a new team that does not know Scrum

**Situation.** A new team has been told to "do Scrum", but nobody has used it before.

**What the coach does.**

1. Runs a short training on the Scrum events and artifacts (teacher stance).
2. Facilitates the first Sprint Planning and Retrospective so the team experiences them.
3. Over the next sprints, steps back: the Scrum Master facilitates, and the coach observes and gives feedback.
4. After a few sprints, the coach asks the team: "What would you like to change in how you work?" and lets the team decide.

*Source: research document, section 9.*

**What the coach does *not* do:** run every event forever, assign tasks, or decide how the team should work.
