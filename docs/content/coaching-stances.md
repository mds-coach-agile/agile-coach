# The 4 Coaching Stances

*Module 03 · The Agile Coaching Institute framework · 6 min read*

## Key takeaways

- An Agile Coach moves between **four stances**: **teacher, mentor, facilitator and professional coach** (Spayd & Adkins, 2011).
- No stance is "best". A good coach **chooses the one that fits the situation**.
- Each stance has a **risk if overused**. The most common is the **teacher trap**: the team waits to be told what to do.
- **Powerful questions** are open, curious and free of hidden advice or blame (Adkins, 2010).

---

## 1. Where the stances come from

The Agile Coaching Competency Framework of the Agile Coaching Institute (Spayd & Adkins, 2011) describes the coach as an agile-lean practitioner who moves between four stances. The stances are supported by areas of mastery: technical, business and transformation.

Two stances **give** something to the person (knowledge or experience). Two stances **draw out** what the person or group already has:

| Gives | Draws out |
| --- | --- |
| Teacher (knowledge) | Facilitator (the group's own decision) |
| Mentor (experience) | Professional coach (the person's own answer) |

*(Grouping written by the project team, based on Spayd & Adkins, 2011.)*

## 2. The four stances at a glance

| Stance | What the coach does | Use it when | Risk if overused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Teacher** | Shares knowledge the team does not have | The team is new to a practice | The team waits to be told |
| **Mentor** | Shares own experience to help someone grow in a role | Someone learns a role the coach knows | The person copies instead of finding a style |
| **Facilitator** | Guides a meeting so the group decides, neutral on content | Planning, retrospectives, team decisions | The team never learns to facilitate |
| **Professional coach** | Asks powerful questions; the person finds the answer | The person has the knowledge but is stuck | Feels like a guessing game for beginners |

*Source: research document, section 4 (Spayd & Adkins, 2011).*

## 3. Each stance in practice

### Teacher

The coach explains a practice the team does not know yet, for example a short training on Scrum events and artifacts for a new team. Then the coach gradually steps back (research document, section 9).

### Mentor

The coach shares their own experience to help someone grow in a role the coach knows well, such as a new Scrum Master preparing their first retrospective. The mentor says "here is what worked for me", and the person decides what fits them.

### Facilitator

The coach guides the *process* of a meeting and stays neutral on the *content*. The group reaches its own decision. Typical moments: Sprint Planning, retrospectives and team decisions (research document, sections 4 and 5).

### Professional coach

The coach asks powerful questions and the person finds the answer. This fits when the person already has the knowledge but is stuck. For a beginner who lacks the knowledge, endless questions feel like a guessing game, so the teacher stance fits better.

## 4. Choosing the right stance

Two questions help a coach choose (*team summary based on the table above*):

1. **Does the person or team have the knowledge?** If not → teacher or mentor. If yes → facilitator or professional coach.
2. **Is it one person or a group?** One person learning a role → mentor or coach. A group deciding together → facilitator.

The stage of the team matters too. Tuckman described four stages of group development: forming, storming, norming and performing (Tuckman, 1965). A coach typically teaches more in forming, facilitates conflict more in storming, and coaches more once the team performs (research document, section 6).

## 5. The teacher trap

The teacher stance is the easiest one: the coach knows the answer, and giving it feels helpful and fast. But if the coach stays in it too long, **the team waits to be told** (research document, section 4). The team stops thinking for itself, and the coach becomes a bottleneck, which is the opposite of the goal in Module 01.

**Signs you are in the teacher trap** *(team summary)*:

- The team asks the coach "What should we do?" before trying anything.
- The coach talks more than the team in retrospectives.
- Nothing changes when the coach is absent, except that decisions wait.

**How to get out:** answer a question with a question ("What options do you see?"), let the team try something imperfect, and switch to the facilitator or professional-coach stance.

A related misconception is "a coach gives answers". That is only true in the teacher and mentor stances; the professional-coach stance asks questions (research document, section 10).

## 6. Powerful questions

Powerful questions are **open**, **curious** and **free of hidden advice or blame** (Adkins, 2010).

| ✅ Powerful | ❌ Avoid | Why to avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| "What options do you see?" | "Did you finish the task?" | Closed: yes/no answer, no reflection |
| "What could we try next sprint?" | "Have you tried pair programming?" | Advice disguised as a question |
| "What would help you most right now?" | "Why did you break the build?" | A "why" that sounds like blame |

*Sources: research document, section 4 (Adkins, 2010). The first two powerful questions come from the research; the others are examples written by the project team.*

## Practical example: a quiet retrospective

**Situation.** In a retrospective, every time a developer raises a problem, the tech lead explains why it was unavoidable. The room goes silent. *(Scenario written by the project team.)*

**Which stance?** The team does not lack knowledge, so teaching does not help. As **facilitator**, the coach can change the format so everyone writes ideas silently first. As **professional coach**, the coach can name what they observe and ask an open question: "I notice it gets quiet after each point. What would make it easier to talk about problems here?"

**Avoid:** a lecture on blameless retrospectives (teacher trap), or telling a story about "how I handled a tech lead once" (mentor stance at the wrong moment).
