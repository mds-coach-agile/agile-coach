# Practise: Conflict on an Agile Team

Conflict is normal on any team. On great agile teams, conflict is even "welcomed as a creative force" (Adkins & Blake, 2009). The question is not *whether* a team has conflict, but **how serious it is** and **what kind of help it needs**. In this section you will meet five realistic situations. For each one, choose what you would do as the team's Agile Coach. You will then see why your choice helps or hurts.

## Before you start: the five levels of conflict

Speed B. Leas described five levels of conflict, from "Level 1: Problem to Solve" to "Level 5: World War", with each level more intense than the one before (Leas, 1985). Lyssa Adkins adapted his model for agile teams (Adkins & Blake, 2009; Adkins, 2010, ch. 9).

| Level | Name | What you notice |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | **Problem to solve** | Everyday conflict. People share information freely and use clear, specific, factual words. "This is the level of constructive disagreement." |
| 2 | **Disagreement** | Self-protection becomes as important as solving the problem. People are "not hostile, just wary". Language moves from specific to general, and people talk offline to others. |
| 3 | **Contest** | The aim is to win. Sides form. Language becomes distorted: overgeneralisations ("always", "never"), either/or thinking and blame. |
| 4 | **Crusade** | People believe the others cannot change and may want them removed from the team. Talk is about ideology and principles, not facts. The attitude is "righteous and punitive". |
| 5 | **World war** | The aim is to destroy the others. "The only option at Level 5 is to separate the combatants." |

Source: Adkins & Blake (2009), based on Leas (1985).

## How does the coach help?

Adkins describes three steps: **learn the model, teach the model to the team, and coach what emerges** (Adkins & Blake, 2009).

- **Teach it first.** Show the team the five levels (for example as a poster on the wall) so they have shared words to talk about conflict.
- **Wait and watch.** When conflict comes up, first see if the team can de-escalate by themselves.
- **Ask, don't fix.** If the team does not de-escalate, the coach can ask: "What level of conflict are you at right now?" and "Is this a useful place to be?" Then the team decides how to de-escalate (Adkins & Blake, 2009).
- **Prepare in advance.** Help the team agree on team norms for conflict, such as "How do we want to be together when conflict arises?" (Adkins & Blake, 2009).
- **Level 5 is different.** At level 5 there is no constructive outcome, and the people involved must be separated (Adkins & Blake, 2009).

The coach's goal is that the team learns to handle conflict itself: "your job is to help them navigate conflict so they can learn to do it themselves" (Adkins & Blake, 2009).

## Scenario 1 – The API debate

**Level of conflict: 1 – Problem to solve**

During the Sprint, Amira and Lucas disagree about how to structure a new API. In the team chat they post links, compare pros and cons, and Amira writes: *"Good point, let's try both options on a small example and see which is cleaner."* The discussion is lively but friendly.

**What do you do as the Agile Coach?**

- **A.** Step in and decide which design to use, so the team does not lose time.
- **B.** Let them continue. Observe, and perhaps later ask them how they handled the disagreement so well. ✅ *Best response*
- **C.** Book a mediation meeting with both developers to discuss their conflict.

**Feedback**

- **A – Not the best choice.** The coach has no authority over technical decisions, and the developers are solving the problem well. Deciding for them takes away their ownership. Scrum Teams are self-managing: "they internally decide who does what, when, and how" (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).
- **B – Best choice.** This is level 1: information is shared freely and the words are clear and factual. It is "the level of constructive disagreement" (Adkins & Blake, 2009). The coach lets the team work. Asking afterwards what went well helps the team notice and repeat good behaviour.
- **C – Too much.** Treating a normal technical discussion as a serious conflict can make people nervous about disagreeing. Teams need constructive disagreement to find good solutions.

**Review:** *The five levels of conflict* (above) · *Agile Coach role – Focus: team autonomy*

**Reflect:** How can you tell the difference between a healthy disagreement and a conflict that needs help?

## Scenario 2 – "It's fine, whatever"

**Level of conflict: 2 – Disagreement**

The tester, Sofia, has twice found that stories marked "done" by the developers were not really tested. In the Daily Scrum she says only: *"Some stories are… let's say, not quite finished. But it's fine, whatever."* The developers look away. After the meeting, Sofia complains about it privately to another tester. Nobody names the problem openly, and the mood has become careful and polite.

**What do you do as the Agile Coach?**

- **A.** Ignore it. Sofia said it was fine, and the team should not waste time in the Daily Scrum.
- **B.** Tell the developers in front of the team that they must test their stories before marking them done.
- **C.** Encourage Sofia to raise the issue directly with the team, and offer to facilitate the conversation in the Retrospective. There, help the team talk about the facts and agree on a shared understanding of "done". ✅ *Best response*

**Feedback**

- **A – Not the best choice.** This is level 2: people are wary, speak in general terms and talk offline to others instead of sharing what they know (Adkins & Blake, 2009). Unresolved problems tend to pile up and can grow into a level 3 contest (Adkins & Blake, 2009).
- **B – Not the best choice.** Blaming people in public makes them defend themselves and pushes the conflict higher. It also makes the coach act like a manager, which is not the coach's role.
- **C – Best choice.** The coach does not carry the complaint for Sofia but encourages her to speak to the team directly (Sinha, 2019). The coach then *facilitates*: the team brings the conversation back to facts and to a shared interest (quality, a clear Definition of Done), and the team owns the solution.

**Review:** *The five levels of conflict* · *Coaching stances – Facilitator* · *Retrospectives (continuous improvement)*

**Reflect:** What signs in people's words or behaviour told you this was more than a level 1 problem?

## Scenario 3 – Front-end vs back-end

**Level of conflict: 3 – Contest**

The Sprint Goal was missed. In the Retrospective, the front-end developers say: *"The back-end people **always** deliver the API too late."* The back-end developers answer: *"Front-end **never** tells us what they need until the last day."* Both groups have been discussing it in separate chats, and each is trying to get the Product Owner on their side.

**What do you do as the Agile Coach?**

- **A.** Ask the Product Owner to decide which group was at fault, so the matter is closed.
- **B.** Pause the discussion and ask the team: "What level of conflict are we at right now? Is this a useful place to be?" Then ask each side for a specific recent example (facts, not "always/never"), and let the team decide how to fix the hand-over, for example agreeing on the API earlier in Sprint Planning. ✅ *Best response*
- **C.** Suggest splitting the team into a front-end team and a back-end team so they stop clashing.

**Feedback**

- **A – Not the best choice.** At level 3, "the aim is to win" (Adkins & Blake, 2009). Naming a group at fault makes one side win and the other lose, so the conflict comes back later. It also gives the Product Owner a role that is not theirs.
- **B – Best choice.** The words "always" and "never", sides forming and people seeking supporters are typical of level 3 (Adkins & Blake, 2009). The coach does not solve it for them. Asking which level they are at, and whether it is useful, helps the team see what is happening and choose to de-escalate themselves (Adkins & Blake, 2009). Moving to concrete facts takes the conversation back towards level 1.
- **C – Not the best choice.** Splitting the team avoids the conflict instead of dealing with it, and it goes against cross-functional teams: the Scrum Guide says Scrum Teams have all the skills needed to create value each Sprint (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).

**Review:** *The five levels of conflict* · *How does the coach help?* · *Facilitation* · *Agile Coach vs Scrum Master vs Manager* (who decides what)

**Reflect:** Which words in the scenario showed you the team had reached level 3?

## Scenario 4 – "He shouldn't be on this team"

**Level of conflict: 4 – Crusade**

For weeks, Daniel and Priya have argued about coding standards. Now Priya tells several colleagues that Daniel *"will never care about quality and should be moved to another team"*. Daniel says Priya *"is the reason this team is toxic"*. Both have asked other team members to choose a side. The disagreement is no longer about coding standards; it is about who is a good or bad person.

**What do you do as the Agile Coach?**

- **A.** Hold an open discussion in the Daily Scrum so the whole team can say what they think of Daniel and Priya.
- **B.** Meet Daniel and Priya separately to listen. Then bring them together in a structured, facilitated conversation with clear ground rules, focused on behaviour and facts, and aiming at a working agreement. If this does not help, involve the Scrum Master and, where needed, their manager or a professional mediator. ✅ *Best response*
- **C.** Tell both of them they are being unprofessional and must stop arguing, or you will report them.

**Feedback**

- **A – Not the best choice.** At level 4, groups harden into factions and people are attacked for which side they are on (Adkins & Blake, 2009). An open discussion with no structure gives both sides an audience and pulls the rest of the team further in.
- **B – Best choice.** At level 4, people believe the others cannot change and may want them removed from the team (Adkins & Blake, 2009). The team is unlikely to de-escalate alone, so the coach adds more structure: private listening first, then a carefully facilitated conversation about facts and behaviour, not about who is "right". Bringing in more formal help when coaching is not enough is part of knowing the limits of the role. *(Project team's note: the step-by-step approach here is our own guidance, built on the level description.)*
- **C – Not the best choice.** Threats do not change how people see each other; they only push the conflict out of sight. The coach also has no authority to discipline people. That is a manager's role.

**Review:** *The five levels of conflict* · *Agile Coach role – Authority* · *Coaching stances*

**Reflect:** Why does the coach need to be more active at level 4 than at level 2?

## Scenario 5 – "Either she goes, or I do"

**Level of conflict: 5 – World war**

After a heated meeting, Tom sends a message to the whole team and the department head: *"Either Elena leaves this team, or I resign today. I'll make sure everyone knows what she's done."* Elena has told a colleague she feels intimidated and does not want to come to work. Other team members are upset and some have stopped speaking to each other.

**What do you do as the Agile Coach?**

- **A.** Bring Tom and Elena together straight away and ask them to talk it through until they agree.
- **B.** Separate Tom and Elena for now so nobody gets hurt, and immediately involve the people with formal authority, such as their manager and HR. Offer support to the rest of the team. ✅ *Best response*
- **C.** Wait until the next Retrospective so the team can discuss it with the usual format.

**Feedback**

- **A – Not the best choice.** At level 5 "the aim … is to destroy the others", and there is "no hope for a constructive outcome" (Adkins & Blake, 2009). Forcing a conversation can make things worse and may not be safe, especially when someone feels intimidated.
- **B – Best choice.** "The only option at Level 5 is to separate the combatants … so that they don't hurt one another" (Adkins & Blake, 2009). The coach has no authority over people, so decisions about the team must be taken by people who do, such as managers and HR. *(Project team's note: involving management and HR is our own guidance, based on the coach's lack of formal authority.)*
- **C – Not the best choice.** Waiting leaves people in an unsafe situation, and a Retrospective is not designed to handle threats or intimidation.

**Review:** *The five levels of conflict* · *Agile Coach vs Scrum Master vs Manager – Authority*

**Reflect:** What is the difference between the coach's role at level 1 and at level 5?

## What did you learn?

- **Diagnose before you act.** First decide which level the conflict is at; the right response depends on it.
- **Teach the model and ask questions.** "What level of conflict are you at right now?" helps the team see the conflict and de-escalate themselves (Adkins & Blake, 2009).
- **Low and middle levels:** the team solves it. The coach observes, asks questions and facilitates, and brings the conversation back to facts and shared interests.
- **Level 4:** the coach adds more structure and knows when to bring in more help.
- **Level 5:** separate the people involved first; there is no constructive outcome at this level (Adkins & Blake, 2009).
- **Never:** blame people in public, take sides, or carry complaints for others; encourage people to speak to each other directly (Sinha, 2019).
