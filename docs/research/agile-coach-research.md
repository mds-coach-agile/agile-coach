# Research: the Agile Coach role

Issue #6 · Sprint 0 · Author: Christian Byemba

This document gathers what we know about the Agile Coach role and where it comes from. It is the content base for the website modules (coaching principles, facilitation, team dynamics, conflict resolution, continuous improvement) and for the BOTSchool.ai chatbot. Every section names its sources; the full list is at the end.

## 1. Summary for the team

- An **Agile Coach** helps teams and organisations work in an agile way and keep improving **on their own**. The coach has no authority over people and does not do the team's work (Adkins, 2010).
- The coach moves between **four stances**: teacher, mentor, facilitator and professional coach, choosing the one that fits the situation (Spayd & Adkins, 2011).
- The role is often confused with the **Scrum Master**. The Scrum Master is a Scrum accountability for one Scrum Team; the Agile Coach works with any agile approach, often across several teams (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).
- The five topics announced in our README are all covered in the literature below, so they can each become a module.

## 2. What an Agile Coach is and does

An Agile Coach helps people find better ways of working, then steps back so the team becomes autonomous. Adkins describes the aim as a team that no longer needs its coach (Adkins, 2010). Davies and Sedley present the coach as someone who helps a team adopt and improve agile practices by working alongside it, not by imposing them (Davies & Sedley, 2009).

Typical activities:

- teaching agile principles and practices (Scrum, Kanban, XP) when the team does not know them yet;
- facilitating events and workshops so the group reaches its own decisions;
- mentoring Scrum Masters, Product Owners and managers learning their role;
- coaching in the strict sense: asking open questions so people find their own answers;
- observing the team and giving feedback;
- working with leaders to remove obstacles outside the team's control.

## 3. Agile Coach, Scrum Master and manager

| | Agile Coach | Scrum Master | Manager |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Scope | One or several teams, sometimes the organisation | One Scrum Team | A team or a department |
| Framework | Any agile approach | Scrum | Not tied to a framework |
| Authority over people | None: influences and supports | None: serves the team | Hierarchical |
| Main goal | Teams that improve on their own | Scrum working well, team effectiveness | Results and people management |

The Scrum Guide 2020 states that the Scrum Master is accountable for establishing Scrum and for the Scrum Team's effectiveness, and describes Scrum Masters as true leaders who serve the Scrum Team and the larger organisation (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). The Scrum Guide does not define an "Agile Coach" role: it is a role from practice and from coaching frameworks, not from Scrum itself. A Scrum Master often uses coaching skills, which explains the confusion.

## 4. Coaching principles: the four stances

The Agile Coaching Competency Framework of the Agile Coaching Institute (Spayd & Adkins, 2011) describes the coach as an agile-lean practitioner who moves between four stances, supported by areas of mastery (technical, business and transformation).

| Stance | What the coach does | Use it when | Risk if overused |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Teacher | Shares knowledge the team does not have | The team is new to a practice | The team waits to be told |
| Mentor | Shares own experience to help someone grow in a role | Someone learns a role the coach knows | The person copies instead of finding a style |
| Facilitator | Guides a meeting so the group decides, neutral on content | Planning, retrospectives, team decisions | The team never learns to facilitate |
| Professional coach | Asks powerful questions; the person finds the answer | The person has the knowledge but is stuck | Feels like a guessing game for beginners |

**Powerful questions** are open, curious and free of hidden advice or blame, for example "What options do you see?" or "What could we try next sprint?". Closed questions, advice disguised as a question ("Have you tried…?") and "why" questions that sound like blame are to be avoided (Adkins, 2010).

## 5. Facilitation

Facilitation helps a group think and decide together while the facilitator stays neutral on the content.

- Kaner et al. describe the **"diamond of participatory decision-making"**: a divergent phase (many ideas), a "groan zone" where the group struggles to understand different views, and a convergent phase (choosing). The facilitator's job is to help the group through the groan zone rather than skip it (Kaner et al., 2014).
- For retrospectives, Derby and Larsen propose five stages: **set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, close the retrospective** (Derby & Larsen, 2006).
- The Scrum Guide time-boxes the events, for example the Daily Scrum at 15 minutes; keeping the timebox is part of the Scrum Master's role (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).

## 6. Team dynamics

- **Stages of group development.** Tuckman described four stages, forming, storming, norming and performing (Tuckman, 1965); a fifth, adjourning, was added later (Tuckman & Jensen, 1977). A coach adapts the stance to the stage: more teaching in forming, more facilitation of conflict in storming, more coaching once the team performs.
- **Psychological safety.** Edmondson defines team psychological safety as a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking, and links it to team learning behaviour (Edmondson, 1999). Retrospectives and experiments only work if people feel safe to speak.
- **Self-management.** The Scrum Guide describes Scrum Teams as self-managing: they decide internally who does what, when and how (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020). The coach helps the team grow into this instead of deciding for it.

## 7. Conflict resolution

Adkins uses the five levels of conflict defined by Speed Leas to judge how serious a conflict is (Adkins, 2010):

1. Problem to solve
2. Disagreement
3. Contest
4. Crusade
5. World war

At the lower levels the coach helps the team solve the conflict itself: listening to each side privately, then facilitating a conversation on shared interests that ends in a working agreement. Blaming in public or escalating too early tends to raise the level.

## 8. Continuous improvement

- The **Sprint Retrospective** exists to plan ways to increase quality and effectiveness; the team identifies the most helpful changes and may add them to the next Sprint Backlog (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).
- Improvement works best in **small experiments**: try one change for one sprint, look at the result, keep or drop it. This is the inspect-and-adapt loop at the heart of Scrum's empiricism (Schwaber & Sutherland, 2020).
- **Metrics** support improvement only when they serve the team. Velocity depends on each team's own estimation scale, so it should not be used to compare or rank teams (Cohn, 2005).

## 9. Typical situations (material for scenarios and the quiz)

| Situation | Good coaching response | Stance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Daily Scrum lasts 40 minutes | Make it visible; let the team redesign it in the retrospective; recall the 15-minute timebox | Facilitator, teacher |
| Two developers clash over code reviews | Talk to each privately, then facilitate a conversation towards a working agreement | Facilitator, coach |
| The Product Owner tells developers how to work | One-to-one talk, start from the PO's concerns, explain self-management, propose a one-sprint experiment | Mentor, coach |
| A manager wants to rank teams by velocity | Ask what the manager needs to know; explain that velocity depends on each team's own estimation scale, so it cannot be compared between teams (Cohn, 2005); propose outcome or flow metrics | Teacher, coach |
| A new team does not know Scrum | Short training on the events and artifacts, then gradually step back | Teacher |

## 10. Common misconceptions to correct on the site

- "The Agile Coach is the team's boss." No: no hierarchical authority.
- "Agile Coach and Scrum Master are the same." No: different scope and origin (section 3).
- "A coach gives answers." Only in the teacher and mentor stances; the professional-coach stance asks questions.
- "Velocity measures performance." No: it is a planning tool for one team, and it depends on that team's own estimation scale, so it cannot be used to compare or rank teams (Cohn, 2005).
