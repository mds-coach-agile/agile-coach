Discover

What is Agile coaching?

An Agile Coach helps teams and organisations work in an agile way and keep improving on their own.

The coach helps people find better ways of working, then steps back so the team becomes autonomous. Lyssa Adkins describes the aim as a team that no longer needs its coach.

What an Agile Coach is not

  • Not the team's boss. The coach has no hierarchical authority over people: they influence and support.
  • Not an extra team member doing the work. The coach works alongside the team, without taking over its work.

What an Agile Coach does

  • Teaches agile principles and practices such as Scrum, Kanban and XP when the team does not know them yet.
  • Facilitates events and workshops so the group reaches its own decisions.
  • Mentors Scrum Masters, Product Owners and managers who are learning their role.
  • Coaches by asking open questions so people find their own answers.
  • Observes the team and gives feedback.
  • Works with leaders to remove obstacles outside the team's control.

    • The Agile Coach works with any agile approach, often across several teams.

  3. Module 3

    The four coaching stances

    Teacher, mentor, facilitator and professional coach: the coach chooses the stance that fits the situation.

    Learn more about the four coaching stances
    • The stances come from the Agile Coaching Competency Framework (Spayd & Adkins, 2011).
    • Each stance helps in some situations and becomes a risk when overused.
    Explore the stances
  4. Module 4

    Facilitation

    Help a group think and decide together while staying neutral on the content.

    Learn more about facilitation
    • Participatory decision-making: a divergent phase, a "groan zone" and a convergent phase. The facilitator helps the group through the groan zone instead of skipping it (Kaner et al., 2014).
    • Effective retrospectives follow five stages: set the stage, gather data, generate insights, decide what to do, close (Derby & Larsen, 2006).
  5. Module 5

    Team dynamics

    How teams develop, why psychological safety matters, and how teams grow into self-management.

    Learn more about team dynamics
    • Forming, storming, norming, performing: Tuckman's stages of group development (1965).
    • Psychological safety: a shared belief that the team is safe for interpersonal risk-taking (Edmondson, 1999).
    • Self-management: the team decides who does what, when and how (Scrum Guide, 2020).
  6. Module 6

    Conflict resolution

    Judge how serious a conflict is with five levels, then help the team resolve it itself.

    Learn more about conflict resolution
    1. Problem to solve
    2. Disagreement
    3. Contest
    4. Crusade
    5. World war

    Speed Leas' five levels of conflict, as used by Adkins (2010).

  7. Module 7

    Continuous improvement

    Retrospectives, small experiments and the inspect-and-adapt loop at the heart of Scrum.

    Learn more about continuous improvement
    • The Sprint Retrospective plans ways to increase quality and effectiveness.
    • Try one change for one sprint, look at the result, then keep it or drop it.
    • Metrics help only when they serve the team: velocity should not be used to rank teams (Cohn, 2005).

Role comparison

Agile Coach, Scrum Master and manager at a glance

A Scrum Master often uses coaching skills, which is why the roles are easily confused. Their scope, framework and authority are different.

Comparison of the Agile Coach, Scrum Master and manager roles
Agile Coach Scrum Master Manager
Scope One or several teams, sometimes the organisation One Scrum Team A team or a department
Framework Any agile approach Scrum Not tied to a framework
Authority over people None: influences and supports None: serves the team Hierarchical
Main goal Teams that improve on their own Scrum working well, team effectiveness Results and people management

Good to know: the Scrum Guide 2020 does not define an "Agile Coach" role. It is a role from practice and from coaching frameworks, not from Scrum itself.

Coaching stances

The four coaching stances

An Agile Coach moves between four stances and chooses the one that fits the situation (Spayd & Adkins, 2011).

  • Teacher

    Shares knowledge the team does not have yet.

    Use it when
    The team is new to a practice.
    Risk if overused
    The team waits to be told.

  • Mentor

    Shares their own experience to help someone grow in a role.

    Use it when
    Someone is learning a role the coach knows.
    Risk if overused
    The person copies instead of finding their own style.

  • Facilitator

    Guides a meeting so the group decides, while staying neutral on the content.

    Use it when
    Planning, retrospectives, team decisions.
    Risk if overused
    The team never learns to facilitate.

  • Professional coach

    Asks powerful questions so the person finds their own answer.

    Use it when
    The person has the knowledge but is stuck.
    Risk if overused
    Feels like a guessing game for beginners.

The stance follows the team

A coach adapts to the team's stage: more teaching while a team is forming, more facilitation of conflict while it is storming, and more coaching once it performs.

Powerful questions

Open, curious and free of hidden advice or blame, for example:

What options do you see? What could we try next sprint?

Why it matters

Why learn Agile coaching?

  • Improve team collaboration

    Help a group think and decide together instead of deciding for it.

  • Develop facilitation skills

    Guide retrospectives and workshops through the groan zone to a decision.

  • Understand team dynamics

    Recognise the stages teams move through and adapt your stance.

  • Handle disagreements constructively

    Judge the level of a conflict and move it towards a working agreement.

  • Support team self-management

    Help teams decide who does what, when and how.

  • Encourage continuous improvement

    Run small experiments, inspect the result and keep what works.
